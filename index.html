    Three threads, one question.

    How do we make computing usable for people it usually leaves out? I work on that question from three sides.

    Blind & low-vision access

    Making block-based programming environments like Google Blockly work with keyboards, screen readers, and audio cues, so visual programming isn't only for sighted students.

    Cognitive accessibility

    Studying how neurodivergent learners experience block-based programming, and building adaptive tools that fit the way they think and focus, rather than the other way round.

    AI and human-computer interaction

    The method behind both: co-design, surveys, usability studies and qualitative analysis, plus AI that adapts to the person, so what gets built is grounded in how people really work.

    • 2026

      AI-AC, an accessibility checker for any website

      A published Chrome extension that scores a page per disability profile instead of one flat list. A local rules engine mapped to WCAG 2.2 runs free; an optional AI vision pass reads a screenshot the way a person sees it and catches text baked into images, meaning carried by colour alone, and mismatched alt text.

    I'm a fourth-year PhD candidate in Computer Science at the University of North Texas, advised by Dr. Stephanie Ludi in the DiscoverABILITY Lab. I expect to graduate in May 2027.

    My research asks a stubborn question: the tools we build for programming and learning assume a certain kind of user, so what does it take to make them work for everyone else? That takes me from screen-reader semantics and audio interfaces to the lived experience of neurodivergent students, and to shipping real tools like AI-AC.

    I move between research methods and engineering because good accessibility needs both: evidence about people, and software that actually runs. I'm from Visakhapatnam, India, and I speak Telugu and English.
